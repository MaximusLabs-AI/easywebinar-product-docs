Where a webinar outline fits

Picture the week before a webinar. You have a title, a date and a registration page, and someone asks what the second half covers. If the answer is "we'll see how it goes", the session will run long and the ask will be rushed. A webinar outline is the one-page plan that answers it. It sets the sections, their order, their length and the moments you ask the audience to act.

What it's for

An outline turns a topic into a running order. It tells you which three ideas you are teaching, how long each one gets, and where the offer and polls sit. The slides and script then follow a plan instead of creating one.

How it helps

  • Time stays honest. Each section has a budget, so you can see an overrun on paper before it happens on air.
  • The offer gets its room. Planning the bridge and the offer as real sections stops them shrinking to a final slide.
  • Polls do a job. Each poll is placed for a reason, such as learning who is attending or gauging interest before the pitch.
  • Writing gets faster. A script, a registration page and follow-up emails are all easier to write from one agreed outline.

What happens without it

Without an outline, the session grows around whatever you most enjoy explaining. Questions get cut, the offer lands when people are already leaving, and the follow-up emails promise things the session never covered.

Who this falls to, and when

It falls to whoever owns the session, usually the presenter or the marketer running the programme. The moment is before any slide or page exists, when moving a section costs nothing.

The EasyWebinar approach to webinar planning

Take a typical case. A two-person product marketing team at a project-management software company is planning its first quarterly webinar for operations managers who are evaluating tools. They have a strong topic, a 45-minute slot and a free trial to offer at the end. They also have three weeks to build everything.

How webinars are structured

There are three common ways to arrange a webinar that ends in an offer. They share the same sections and differ in how early, and how often, the offer appears. Choosing one before you fill in the template decides how long sections 6 and 7 need to be.

Teach first, offer at the end

The whole first part is teaching, and the offer comes once, near the close. It builds trust well and suits a cold audience meeting you for the first time. What it misses is attention: if the teaching overruns, the offer arrives after people have left.

Value with the offer woven through

The offer is mentioned lightly during the teaching, then made properly at the end. Attendees are not surprised by it, and the close can be stronger. The risk is that early mentions feel like interruptions if they are not tied to the point you just made.

Offer-led, with repeated calls to action

The session is built around the offer, with several calls to action and a clear deadline. It suits a warm audience that already knows the product. It misses people who came to learn, who may feel sold to before they have been taught anything.

Answer first, inside every section

Whichever arrangement you choose, open each section with its main point and then support it. This is the inverted-pyramid idea from writing, where the most important information comes first and detail follows. On a webinar it means an attendee who drifts for two minutes still knows what each section was about.

What each section of the outline should do

The template names eight sections. This is what each one has to achieve, and what usually goes wrong in it.

Opening and housekeeping

Welcome people, say who you are in a sentence and set the rules of the room. The common mistake is a long biography. Attendees came for the promise, so keep your introduction short and let your teaching earn the credibility.

The promise

State the one result attendees will leave with, then name your three teaching points. This is the agenda in spoken form. If you cannot write the promise as one sentence starting "By the end you'll be able to", the topic is still too wide.

Three teaching points

Three points are enough for 45 or 60 minutes. Each one gets an idea, an example and a takeaway. Research on memory suggests people hold a small number of chunks at once, which is a good reason to resist a fourth or fifth point. Put the point that leads most naturally to your offer last.

The bridge

The bridge is the two or three minutes between teaching and selling. Sum up what attendees can now do alone, then name what is still hard. Your offer should close exactly that gap. Without a bridge, the pitch feels like a change of subject.

The offer

Say what it is, who it is for, what is included and the single next step. Write the button text before the session, because it should say what happens when someone clicks. If the offer has a deadline, decide now whether it carries a countdown.

Questions and close

Protect this time in the plan. Answer live questions, repeat the next step once, and tell people what will arrive by email afterwards. If you are running short, cut teaching, not questions, because questions are often where buyers raise the objections your offer has to answer.

Where polls and the offer belong in a webinar

Polls work best with a job to do. Plan each one against a section in the outline, not as a break when energy dips.

  1. Poll A, in the opening. Ask who is in the room: role, company size or how far along they are. It warms people up and tells you which examples to stress.
  2. Poll B, after teaching point 2. Ask about their experience with the problem. The answers give you a live example to use in point 3.
  3. Poll C, in the bridge. Ask how interested people are in going further. It gauges interest in your offer just before you make it.

The offer itself belongs after the bridge and before the questions. Placing it there means the questions can deal with objections while the offer is still on screen. On a simulive or evergreen webinar the same order holds, because polls and offers appear at the points you set rather than when you remember them.

What goes on a webinar housekeeping slide

The housekeeping slide sits in section 1 and answers the questions people would otherwise type into chat. Keep it to four lines:

  • How to ask a question, and whether you will answer during the session or at the end.
  • How long the session runs, so people can plan to stay for the offer and the questions.
  • Whether there is a replay, and how people will get it.
  • Where to click when you share a link, if you plan to show an offer or a download.

Say these out loud as well as showing them, because some people join late and miss the slide. If you take questions in a separate panel, point to it now; the questions and answers page covers how that panel works.

Outline mistakes to avoid

  • Timing only the teaching. If the bridge, the offer and the questions have no minutes against them, they lose out first.
  • Five teaching points in 45 minutes. Each point gets too little time to land, and the offer comes late.
  • An offer with no bridge. The pitch reads as a change of subject instead of the next step.
  • Polls with no purpose. A poll that tells you nothing you will act on wastes a minute of the session.
  • Emails that promise a different session. Write the registration page and follow-ups from the finished outline, not the first idea.

How to create a webinar outline by hand

  1. Write the audience in one specific sentence, such as "operations managers comparing project tools", not "managers".
  2. Write the one result they leave with, starting "By the end you'll be able to".
  3. List every idea you could teach, then keep the three that lead most directly to that result.
  4. Order the three so the last one leads naturally to your offer.
  5. Choose an arrangement: teach first, value throughout or offer-led.
  6. Fill in the minutes column of the template so the total matches your slot exactly.
  7. Write the bridge sentence: what attendees can do now, and what is still hard.
  8. Write the offer in four lines: what it is, who it is for, what is included and the button text.
  9. Place three polls against sections 1, 4 and 6, each with its purpose written beside it.
  10. Read the outline aloud with a timer and move minutes until the questions still have their time.

Check whether the outline worked

Two measures tell you whether the plan held up, and both come from the sections you timed.

Offer views to clicks

Divide offer clicks by offer views. A low figure means the offer itself is not landing, which often points back to a thin bridge or an offer that arrived too late. Its weak spot is that it says nothing about checkout: a strong click rate with few purchases is a checkout or pricing problem, not an outline problem.

Questions asked and answered

Compare how many questions were asked with how many you answered. If many go unanswered, section 8 was squeezed, and the usual cause is an overrun in the teaching points. Its weak spot is that a quiet audience asks little, so a low count can mean disinterest rather than a well-timed session.

Common questions about webinar outlines

What sections should a webinar have?

A webinar that teaches and then sells needs eight sections: opening and housekeeping, the promise, three teaching points, a bridge, the offer, and questions with a close. Each one gets a time budget so the offer and questions keep their room.

Where in a webinar should the offer go?

Put the offer after your last teaching point, through a short bridge that names the gap it closes. Leave time for questions afterwards, because that is where attendees raise the objections the offer has to answer.

How long should each section of a webinar run?

In a 45-minute session, try 4 minutes of opening, 2 for the promise, 8 for each of three teaching points, 2 for the bridge, 6 for the offer and 7 for questions. Adjust the split, but keep the total fixed.

What goes on a webinar housekeeping slide?

Four things: how to ask a question, how long the session runs, whether there is a replay, and where to click when you share a link. Say them aloud too, because people who join late miss the slide.

What is the difference between a webinar outline, agenda and script?

The outline is your private plan of sections, timings and asks. The agenda is the public version you promise attendees. The script is the word-for-word talk written from the outline, slide by slide.

How many teaching points should a webinar have?

Three is enough for 45 or 60 minutes. Each point needs an idea, an example and a takeaway, and more points leave each one too little time while pushing the offer later.

Where should polls go in a webinar?

Plan one poll in the opening to learn who is attending, one after the second teaching point about their experience, and one in the bridge to gauge interest in going further before you make the offer.

Can EasyWebinar write my webinar outline for me?

Does the same outline work for a simulive or evergreen webinar?

Yes. The sections and timings stay the same, and you set the webinar type before generating. A simulive or evergreen webinar then needs a video chosen afterwards.

Will my polls and offers appear on the replay?

They do when the replay page uses Webinar Replica, which duplicates the live webinar with the same video, offers, polls and chat replay. Your outline's asks then keep working for people who watch later.

Should my webinar offer have a countdown?

Only if the offer has a real deadline. In EasyWebinar, switching on Countdown timer in the offer editor puts a countdown on the offer card, so decide this in the outline before you build the offer.

How do I know my webinar outline worked?

Check offer views against clicks and questions asked against questions answered. A low click rate points to the bridge or the offer, and many unanswered questions point to teaching that overran.

How do I stop my webinar running over time?

Give every section a fixed end time in the outline, treat questions as protected time, and rehearse with a timer. If you run long on the day, shorten the last teaching point rather than the offer or questions.
